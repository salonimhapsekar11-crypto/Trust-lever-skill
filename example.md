# Worked Examples — Trust Lever Audit

Three condensed examples showing how the framework applies across brand types and categories. These are quality references for calibration, not templates to copy verbatim — never block an audit run on reading this file.

---

## Example 1 — Challenger D2C skincare brand

**Assumed audience:** Sceptical, ingredient-literate buyer in her late 20s–30s researching actives for the first time.
**Brand type:** Challenger (Route A — founder story).

| Lever | Score | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Authenticity | ●●●●○ 4 | Founder story present and specific; formulator credentials shown but not linked to verification |
| Social Proof | ●●●○○ 3 | Reviews present but unattributed — no verified-purchase tag |
| Transparency | ●●●●● 5 | Full INCI list with concentrations on every product card |
| Familiarity | ●●●○○ 3 | Imagery skews aspirational/retouched, mismatched with ingredient-literate audience |
| Risk & Safety | ●●○○○ 2 | No guarantee or return policy visible above the fold |

**Total: 17/25 — Trust-developing.**

**Sample quick win (not a typo fix):** Return/guarantee policy exists but lives only in footer legal text. A product designer could surface a guarantee badge near the primary CTA — this is a placement and hierarchy problem, not a copy problem.

## Example 2 — Scaled probiotics brand

**Assumed audience:** Health-conscious buyer, some scepticism about supplement efficacy claims generally.
**Brand type:** Scaled (Route B — institutional).

| Lever | Score | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Authenticity | ●●●●○ 4 | Longevity and unit-shipped numbers stated; no named formulator, appropriate for scaled brand |
| Social Proof | ●●●●○ 4 | Goal-specific reviews ("helped my bloating") outperform generic praise |
| Transparency | ●●○○○ 2 | Strain names present but CFU counts buried in an accordion |
| Familiarity | ●●●●○ 4 | Tone and layout reduce cognitive load appropriately |
| Risk & Safety | ●●●○○ 3 | Third-party testing mentioned in text, no certification badge shown |

**Total: 17/25 — Trust-developing.**

**Sample quick win:** CFU counts and strain specificity are the category's expected transparency signal but require an extra click to find. A designer could move this from the accordion to the primary product card — a layout decision, not a content rewrite.

## Example 3 — Health tech (digital diagnostics)

**Assumed audience:** Anxious buyer evaluating a health decision, not a casual browser.
**Brand type:** Scaled (Route B).

| Lever | Score | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Authenticity | ●●●○○ 3 | Clinical partnerships named but not linked to verifiable sources |
| Social Proof | ●●○○○ 2 | Testimonials present, no outcome specificity |
| Transparency | ●●●●○ 4 | Data privacy policy is clear and linked prominently |
| Familiarity | ●●○○○ 2 | Clinical, sterile tone increases anxiety rather than reducing it |
| Risk & Safety | ●●●●○ 4 | Peer-reviewed citations present and linked |

**Total: 15/25 — Trust-developing (borderline at risk given high-anxiety audience).**

**Sample quick win:** Tone and layout are clinically accurate but not reassuring — a designer could redesign the results-explanation flow to reduce jargon density and add a plain-language summary layer, addressing Familiarity without touching the clinical content itself.

---

## What These Examples Illustrate
- Route A vs Route B scoring changes what "good" looks like for Authenticity — do not penalise a scaled brand for lacking a founder.
- A 17/25 total can mean very different things depending on which levers are weak — always read the per-lever pattern, not just the sum.
- Quick wins in all three examples are placement, hierarchy, or flow problems a designer can act on — never spelling or grammar fixes.
